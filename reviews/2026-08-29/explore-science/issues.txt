1. Manuscript Summary
Untapped Capital researchers demonstrate that assertions of "deterministic replay" and
"cheap forking" in event-sourced agents mask severe risks regarding external interactions
and schedule confluence. By formalizing replay as a property-indexed family of
assertions-validated via an executable contract against 5,540 runtime logs-they establish that
trace sharing alone cannot ensure safe execution branching. This converts agent forking
from a basic storage optimization into a precise semantic contract, preventing unintended
external actions when recovering or diverging autonomous system states.

3. Summary of Notable Merits
This manuscript presents a body of technical work distinguished by the depth and
consistency of its formal rigor, empirical transparency, and reporting discipline across
nearly every dimension the review examined. Two patterns stand out: a systematic
insistence on grounding every theoretical construct in directly auditable evidence, and a
sustained commitment to calibrating claims precisely to what the evidence supports -
neither inflating results nor discarding genuinely qualified prior insights. Together, these
qualities exceed prevailing standards in agent runtime research, where formal properties
are rarely bound to falsifiable empirical witnesses and claim boundaries are seldom
explicitly maintained.

The three most notable merits identified were:

   •   A three-dimensional orthogonal effect taxonomy (External Footprint, Replay Source,
       Lifecycle) that enables property-indexed safety obligations, resolving a conflation
       embedded in flat categorizations used by deployed systems such as Temporal and
       Restate.
   •   A four-tier progressive fork safety property family (ProjectionReplay through
       CounterfactualWorld) that converts forking from a storage mechanism into an
       explicit semantic contract, far exceeding binary fork-capability treatments standard
       in the field.
   •   A zero-margin numerical reconciliation of lifecycle boundary unsoundness against
       structural execution gaps, providing closed-form empirical proof where comparable
       work typically relies on sampling or informal case studies.
Collectively, these strengths substantially elevate the manuscript's inferential authority and
position it as a methodological reference point for the field.

4. Review Synopsis
The manuscript is in strong overall condition, with revision needs that are narrow in scope
but meaningful for consolidating the formal contributions at its core. Two themes recur
across the issues: gaps in how formal elements are connected to one another within the
manuscript's own terms, and uneven derivational depth in the empirical reporting - the
latter exemplified by asymmetric treatment of the conditional cut category. Together, these
patterns modestly reduce the reader's ability to verify that the formal property family and
the assessment results are as tightly integrated as claimed, which matters precisely because
the manuscript's authority rests on the closed-form, auditable character of its evidence.
The three highest-priority issues identified in the review were:

  •   the missing explicit mapping between the seven-tier assertion hierarchy and the four
      fork properties (#A1)
  •   the unaddressed timing race in the synchronous log-fold assessment design (#B1)
  •   the absent structural derivation for the largest single count in Table 5 (#C1)
Prioritizing the formal integration gap in #A1 and the design boundary in #B1 would most
directly strengthen confidence in the manuscript's central claims.

5.1 Introduction
  #A1. Cross-Mapping Between Foundational Assertion Hierarchy and
  Operationalized Fork Properties
  Critique:
  The paper's seven-tier assertion hierarchy in Figure 1 and the four formally
  operationalized fork properties (Definitions 3–6) are never explicitly mapped to one
  another, leaving the manuscript's central organizing claim structurally incomplete.
  Justification & Consequence:
  Assertions such as Path are absent from all subsequent formal treatment, and
  CounterfactualWorld lacks an explicit Figure 1 counterpart, while Environment maps
  ambiguously to both ExternalContinuation and CounterfactualWorld. The 3 artifact
  equivalences, 5 replay grades, and 3 effect dimensions introduced across Sections 2.4, 4,
  and 5 further compound this without cross-referencing. Readers cannot trace whether
  empirical results validate the foundational promises of the introduction, undermining
  the coherence of Contribution 1. Although Decision appears tangentially in Section 2.4
  and Section 7.1, it is never formally situated within the fork property framework.
  Relevant Section:
      Figure 1 and its caption (p. 2); Definitions 3–6 (pp. 9–10); Section 2.4 observation
      equivalences (p. 5); Section 5 replay grades (pp. 11–13); and the Introduction's
      contributions list (pp. 2–3).

  Framework for Addressing:
  •   Add a concise mapping paragraph at the end of Section 1 (or a dedicated subsection of
      Section 2 or 4) that explicitly aligns each of the seven Figure 1 assertions to the
      corresponding fork property, equivalence relation, and/or replay grade-even if the
      mapping is one-to-many or acknowledges that Path and Decision are subsumed
      rather than independently formalized.
  •   Where assertions from Figure 1 are deliberately not operationalized (e.g., Path), state
      this explicitly with a one-sentence justification so readers understand the scope
      boundary is intentional rather than an oversight.


5.2 Methods
  #B1. Asynchronous Hazard Latency in Cut-Level Fork Assessment
  Critique:
  The synchronous log-fold design means a fork assessed as Sound at cut k may precede an
  asynchronous hazard event that is only appended at position k+1 after the assessment
  completes, a timing race the current model does not address.




                                                                                             Page 6
                                                                    agent-runtime-laws-paper-draft




Justification & Consequence:
In deployed event-sourced systems, external effect failures-webhook timeouts,
asynchronous compensations-are commonly recorded with non-trivial latency relative
to the triggering operation . The paper's assessor folds a fixed log snapshot, so a
`EvidenceRecorded(HazardDetected)` event arriving after assessment time is invisible to
the verdict, producing a spurious Sound ruling for ExternalContinuation or
CounterfactualWorld. As the authors note in their limitations, adapter-coverage
assumptions can be invalidated by new event types, but the narrower race between
assessment time and hazard-append time is not discussed.
Relevant Section:
    Counterexample 4 and grade-downgrade discussion (p. 12); Definitions 4–5 (pp. 9–10);
    Section 10 limitations on adapter assumptions (pp. 22–23).

Framework for Addressing:
•   Add a brief paragraph in Section 10 (Limitations) explicitly naming the
    assessment-time/hazard-append race as a boundary condition: the assessor's Sound
    verdict is relative to the log snapshot at assessment time, and production use requires
    a freshness window or quiescence guarantee before the assessment is trusted.
•   In Definition 5 or its accompanying remark, note that ExternalContinuation verdicts
    carry an implicit "log closed at assessment time" premise.

#B2. Notational Overloading of Variable L Across Fixed Log and Mutable
Trace Roles
Critique:
Variable L serves two distinct roles-a fixed input event log in projection and fork
equations and a mutable retained-trace component within the configuration
tuple-without distinguishing notation, which could be clarified for formal precision.
Justification & Consequence:
Equations 4, 6, and 7 treat L as a completed immutable sequence, while the
PROCESS-EVENT rule in Figure 2 accumulates L via append during settlement. Under the
non-confluent schedules of Section 3, the two can differ, so the propositions' scope
depends on which reading applies. Introducing a lightweight notational distinction-e.g.,
\(\hat{L}\) or \(L_{\text{ret}}\) for the configuration component-would remove this
ambiguity without restructuring any proof.
Relevant Section:
    Log definition and projection equation (p. 3–4); configuration definition and
    PROCESS-EVENT rule (pp. 4–5, Figure 2); fork and projection propositions (pp. 9–10).

Framework for Addressing:
•   Introduce a distinct symbol (e.g., \(L_{\text{ret}}\)) for the retained-trace field of the
    configuration tuple at its first appearance in Section 2.3, and use it consistently in
    Figure 2 and Section 2.3 prose, reserving plain L for the fixed input log throughout
    Sections 2.1, 4, and 5.

#B3. FsCheck Parameterization and Test Composition Reporting
Critique:
The artifact description reports "50 passing tests" without disclosing the MaxTest
iteration count, random seed strategy, or the split between static named fixtures and
FsCheck properties, leaving the combinatorial coverage of the property-based checks
unquantifiable.
Justification & Consequence:
FsCheck's default MaxTest of 100 inputs per property means that across a
60-combination effect-dimension space, roughly 8–9 combinations go unsampled per
property run under birthday-problem expectations. Without knowing MaxTest and the
fixture/property ratio, readers cannot assess whether the verdict-matrix completeness
claims in Section 4.2 are supported by adequate generation depth. The artifact's
reproducibility discipline in Section 6.5 covers revision pinning and hash attestation but
does not extend to these generator parameters.
Relevant Section:
    Artifact structure listing 50 passing tests (p. 13, Section 6.1); property-based checks
    description (pp. 13–14, Section 6.2); reproducibility discipline (p. 15, Section 6.5).

Framework for Addressing:
•   Add a short sentence in Section 6.2 reporting the FsCheck MaxTest setting used,
    whether a fixed seed was applied for regression, and the count of static named
    fixtures versus live FsCheck properties among the 50 tests.
•   If MaxTest was left at the default 100, note this explicitly so readers can judge
    combinatorial exposure against the effect-dimension space described in Section 4.1.

#B4. Self-Containment of Formal Definition 5
Critique:
Definition 5 (ExternalContinuation) embeds implementation-specific scope limitations
and a forward reference to Section 7 inside the definition body, mixing normative
property content with artifact-specific hedges.
Justification & Consequence:
Formal definitions in this genre should be self-contained normative statements; notes
about what the v0 assessor does or does not do are editorial and belong in a following
Remark or paragraph (Pierce, 2002). As written, a reader cannot evaluate the property
without also parsing Section 7, and the scope limitation (the bridge does not validate
assertion contents) risks being misread as a constraint of the property rather than the
implementation, weakening the definitional clarity the paper's abstract relies on.
Relevant Section:
    Definition 5 body (p. 9, Section 4.2).

Framework for Addressing:
•   Restructure Definition 5 to contain only the normative property statement, then
    immediately follow it with a clearly labeled Remark that notes the v0 assessor's
    target-environment premise, the bridge's scope limitation, and the Section 7 forward
    reference.

  #B5. Combinatorial Coverage of Language-Neutral Conformance Vectors
  Critique:
  Seven language-neutral JSON conformance vectors provide a useful portability baseline
  but are quantitatively insufficient to cover the full verdict cross-product across the four
  fork properties and effect dimension space described in Sections 4–5.
  Justification & Consequence:
  The authors commendably provide vectors alongside a JSON Schema so that other
  runtimes can challenge the semantics; however, four properties × three lifecycle states ×
  two replay sources yields 24 boundary cases before accounting for the five distinct
  Conditional obligation kinds (Equations 10–14) or CounterfactualWorld's Ω-dependent
  variants. Seven vectors cannot cover this space, and no mapping from vectors to
  boundary cases is provided, limiting the vectors' utility for comprehensive
  cross-implementation conformance testing beyond the baseline case.
  Relevant Section:
      Artifact structure listing seven conformance vectors (p. 13, Section 6.1); operational
      recommendations citing them for cross-implementation use (p. 22, Section 9.6).

  Framework for Addressing:
  •   Add a brief table or list in Section 6.1 enumerating which boundary case each of the
      seven vectors is intended to exercise, making the coverage scope explicit.
  •   Acknowledge in the Limitations section that the current seven vectors constitute an
      initial baseline rather than exhaustive conformance coverage, and identify which
      obligation types from Equations 10–14 lack a dedicated vector.


5.3 Results and Analysis
  #C1. Mechanistic Derivation of the CounterfactualWorld Unsound Cut Count
  Critique:
  The 142,894 CW Unsound count-the largest single result in Table 5-warrants a structural
  derivation comparable to those provided for the ExternalContinuation Sound and
  Unsound categories.
  Justification & Consequence:
  For a run with r completed oracle calls at positions p1 < … < pᵣ, every cut from 0 through
  pᵣ − 1 falls in the CW Unsound region, producing a count governed by the cumulative
  prefix length before each run's final oracle response. This mechanism explains why CW
  Unsound (~4× EC Unsound) is geometrically determined by oracle-call position
  distributions, not merely the 4,923 affected-run count the paper cites. The paper
  provides explicit arithmetic derivations for both EC categories but offers none here,
  creating an asymmetry in auditability for the methodology claimed in Section 6.4.
  Relevant Section:
      Table 5 and its surrounding discussion (pp. 16–17, Section 7.1.2), specifically the
      sentence attributing 4,923 runs to CW Unsound cuts.

Framework for Addressing:
•   Add one to two sentences in Section 7.1.2 after the 4,923-run count explaining that the
    142,894 figure equals the sum across affected runs of (cut of last oracle response) - i.e.,
    all prefix positions before the final committed oracle event contribute a CW Unsound
    cut - providing a structural cross-check analogous to the EC derivation already in the
    text.
•   If space is limited, a footnote with the per-run arithmetic identity is sufficient.

#C2. Structural Account of the EC Conditional Cut Count
Critique:
The 120,969 EC Conditional count, representing 38% of all 317,296 cuts, receives no
per-event structural derivation equivalent to those provided for the Sound and Unsound
EC categories.
Justification & Consequence:
The text derives the 36,251 EC Unsound cuts event-by-event and confirms 160,076 EC
Sound cuts via a direct ordinal query, creating an expectation that the 120,969 EC
Conditional count-recoverable as a residual-would receive analogous treatment. No
explanation is given for why 36,227 committed oracle responses generate approximately
3.34 Conditional cuts each, nor which event positions within a typical run produce them.
This ratio reflects how many prefix positions inherit a committed OneShot oracle result
in their retained prefix, a mechanism the Section 4.3–4.4 cut analysis treats but the
empirical section does not explicitly connect to the count.
Relevant Section:
    Table 5 and EC category discussion (pp. 16–17, Section 7.1.2); cut-through-effects
    analysis (pp. 10–11, Section 4.4).

Framework for Addressing:
•   Extend the Section 7.1.2 derivation paragraph with one sentence explaining that EC
    Conditional cuts are those prefix positions after a committed oracle response, making
    the ~3.34 cuts-per-response ratio a function of the average number of events
    following each oracle response within the same run.
•   This connects the Section 4.4 post-committed-OneShot case explicitly to the Table 5
    count, completing the structural audit trail for all three EC categories.

#C3. Capitalization Consistency of Verdict Terminology
Critique:
Inconsistent capitalization of "Sound" as both a title-case verdict token and a lowercase
general adjective throughout the manuscript could be refined for terminological
precision.
Justification & Consequence:
Where "Sound" denotes the closed programmatic verdict enumeration returned by the
assessor (alongside "Conditional" and "Unsound"), consistent title-casing distinguishes it
from the ordinary-language adjective "sound" used in constructions such as "sound
under the specified observation." While context reliably disambiguates the intended
meaning, uniform typographic convention would prevent any potential conflation of a
system-returned verdict with a universal correctness claim.

5.4 Figures and Tables
  #E1. In-Text Anchoring and Labeling Consistency of Tabular Displays
  Critique:
  Figure 3 and Figure 4 lack explicit numerical citations in the preceding prose, and both
  present row-and-column categorical data that is structurally more consistent with table
  formatting conventions than figure conventions.
  Justification & Consequence:
  Standard practice in formal technical manuscripts requires every numbered display to
  be explicitly anchored in the text before or at the point of appearance (Zobel, 2014).
  Neither Figure 3 nor Figure 4 is introduced by a phrase such as "see Figure 3" in the body
  text; readers must infer their relevance from proximity. Additionally, labeling
  row-and-column categorical summaries as figures rather than tables departs from the
  display-type distinction maintained for Figure 1, Figure 2, and Tables 1–5 elsewhere in
  the manuscript.
  Relevant Section:
      Figure 3 caption and surrounding prose (p. 7, Section 3.2); Figure 4 caption and
      surrounding prose (p. 11, Section 4.4).

  Framework for Addressing:
  •   Relabel Figure 3 and Figure 4 as Table 6 and Table 7 (or whichever numbers continue
      the existing sequence) to reflect their tabular structure.
  •   Insert explicit in-text citations (e.g., "(see Table 6)" and "(see Table 7)") in the
      immediately preceding prose sentences in Sections 3.2 and 4.4 respectively.
